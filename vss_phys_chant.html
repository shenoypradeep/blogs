The Physiology of
Vishnu Sahasranāma Chanting

What happens inside the body when thousands of names are recited — one breath, one śloka, one rhythm at a time?

When you chant the Vishnu Sahasranāma Stotram, something remarkable is happening beneath the words.

You are not merely producing Sanskrit syllables. You are repeatedly coordinating breath, diaphragm, vocal folds, exhalation, heart rhythm, attention and the autonomic nervous system. The structure of the śloka and its chandas create recurring syllabic and rhythmic units; those units help organize the breath; the breath powers the voice; the voice engages the laryngeal and auditory systems; and the autonomic nervous system is continuously communicating with the organs of the body.

This gives us four particularly interesting physiological dimensions of chanting:

Chanting is therefore best understood as an integrated respiratory–phonatory–autonomic practice, not as a single “vagus nerve trick.”

Start with the chant itself

The key connection: The diaphragm does not suddenly appear as a separate “breathing technique” added to Vishnu Sahasranāma. It is already part of the respiratory machinery that makes sustained chanting possible. The chandas supplies temporal and syllabic structure; the respiratory system supplies and meters airflow; the larynx turns airflow into sound; and the resulting respiratory–vocal rhythm participates in autonomic regulation.

The Chandas: the architecture of the Śloka
How the structure of Vishnu Sahasranāma organizes breath, sound and rhythm

Before we look at the physiology, look at the Śloka.

Vishnu Sahasranāma is not a stream of words arranged randomly. Its verses are built within the disciplined architecture of chandas — meter. For the chanter, that meter is not merely something to be analysed after the fact. It gives the śloka a repeatable temporal and syllabic structure.

The great majority of the Vishnu Sahasranāma Stotram is presented in Anuṣṭubh chandas. An Anuṣṭubh śloka consists of four pādas (quarters), with eight syllables in each pāda — a total of 32 syllables in the standard form.

Anuṣṭubh 1 śloka → 4 pādas → 8 syllables per pāda → 32 syllables
The four-pāda structure gives the chanter a recurring rhythmic framework within which breath, articulation and vocal delivery can be coordinated.

See the structure inside an actual Vishnu Sahasranāma śloka

That regularity is precisely what makes chandas so interesting from a physiological perspective. The chanter repeatedly encounters four predictable units. Each unit has a defined syllabic architecture; each syllable requires articulation; the sequence is carried by the breath; and the complete śloka resolves into the next cycle.

Chandas does not “tell the lungs when to breathe” — it creates the framework within which breathing is coordinated.

This distinction is important. A meter is not a respiratory command. The exact place at which a particular chanter inhales depends on the traditional mode of recitation, speed, pronunciation, individual breath capacity and the way the śloka is taught.

But the regularity of the chandas means that the respiratory task is not arbitrary. As you repeatedly chant the same ślokas, the relationship between syllables, vocalization, exhalation and inhalation becomes increasingly predictable.

Thus, in chanting, chandas becomes an organizing framework for breath and voice. The physiological response emerges from the interaction of this structure with the way the śloka is actually recited.

Why this matters for Vishnu Sahasranāma

Vishnu Sahasranāma contains 108 ślokas of the main sahasranāma sequence. The chanter therefore encounters the same basic metrical architecture again and again — not once, but hundreds of times across a sustained practice.

Over repeated recitation, the body learns the relationship between syllable → articulation → airflow → exhalation → śloka completion → next inhalation.

This is why it is useful to say that chandas regulates the breath — provided we understand “regulates” in the physiological sense of organizing and constraining the timing of vocal respiration, rather than claiming that every Anuṣṭubh śloka mechanically produces one identical breathing pattern.

01 · Diaphragmatic Breathing
The breath behind the chant

Where does this happen in the chant? Between vocalized portions of the śloka, the chanter replenishes the air supply. That inhalation is generated largely by the diaphragm: it contracts and descends, increasing thoracic volume and drawing air into the lungs. Then, while the śloka is recited, outgoing air becomes the driving force for phonation. So diaphragmatic breathing is not “after” chanting or “before” chanting — it is part of the respiratory cycle that continuously supports chanting.
{needle3}

Every spoken or chanted śloka is carried by breath. As a śloka unfolds, that air has to be delivered with remarkable control.

The principal muscle of inspiration is the diaphragm — a dome-shaped sheet of muscle separating the chest from the abdomen. When it contracts, it descends and expands the thoracic cavity, helping draw air into the lungs. When it relaxes, it rises again.

What people commonly call “belly breathing” is therefore not air entering the belly. The abdominal wall moves because the descending diaphragm changes the pressure and shape of the thoraco-abdominal system.

Cleveland Clinic's explanation of diaphragmatic breathing describes the technique as a way of using the diaphragm more effectively and notes potential effects including relaxation and reductions in heart rate and blood pressure. Johns Hopkins Medicine similarly teaches diaphragmatic breathing as a relaxation and pain-management technique.

Why is this especially relevant to chanting?

Because chanting is expiration with a purpose.

In ordinary quiet breathing, expiration is often passive. In vocalization, however, the outgoing breath becomes the raw material for sound. Air from the lungs creates subglottic pressure; the vocal folds convert that airflow into vibration; the resulting sound is shaped by the vocal tract.

A recent review of the neural control of vocalization describes this coordination explicitly: voice production requires precise and sustained exhalation, with the respiratory and laryngeal systems working together through brainstem and higher neural circuits. González-García et al., 2024

In the chant Think of each śloka as a small respiratory exercise. You inhale enough to begin the śloka, then meter the outgoing air so that the sound remains stable until the śloka is complete. The better the breath control, the less the chanter needs to gasp, strain or interrupt the flow.

What does research say about diaphragmatic breathing?

A quantitative systematic review examined diaphragmatic breathing as an isolated intervention for physiological and psychological stress. Only three studies met the review's criteria, so the evidence base was small; nevertheless, all three studies reported beneficial effects. Outcomes included respiratory rate, salivary cortisol, blood pressure and perceived stress. The authors concluded that diaphragmatic breathing may reduce physiological and psychological stress, while calling for further research. Hopper et al., 2019 — systematic review

That makes a modest but important point: the breathing pattern itself can influence the body's stress physiology.

How do you recognize good diaphragmatic breathing while chanting?

  • The lower ribs and abdomen move comfortably rather than the shoulders lifting dramatically.
  • The inhalation is quiet and sufficient — not a huge gulp of air.
  • The exhalation is controlled rather than dumped out immediately.
  • The voice remains steady as the śloka progresses.
  • The throat, jaw and face remain relatively relaxed.

Cleveland Clinic and Johns Hopkins both emphasize relaxed, controlled breathing rather than forceful “maximum” inhalations.

02 · Longer Exhales
The hidden rhythm inside a śloka

Listen to a complete śloka being chanted.

There is a pattern hiding in plain sight:

Inhale → begin the śloka → sustain the sound → gradually spend the breath → complete the śloka/pāda → inhale again.

The exhalation used to carry a śloka or its pādas may be much longer than the brief inhalation that precedes it, depending on the recitation style and the chanter.

That matters because respiration and cardiovascular function are not independent systems. Every breath produces mechanical and neural changes that influence the heart and blood vessels.

Respiratory sinus arrhythmia: your heart follows your breath

During normal breathing, heart rate tends to rise during inspiration and fall during expiration. This phenomenon is called respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA).

Slow breathing makes this interaction more pronounced. A major review of slow breathing describes effects on respiratory muscle activity, ventilation, heart-rate variability (HRV), blood-pressure oscillations, baroreflex sensitivity, cardiorespiratory coupling and RSA. Russo et al., 2017

Why does approximately 6 breaths per minute keep appearing in the research?

Because for many adults, breathing around 6 times per minute brings the respiratory rhythm close to a frequency at which several cardiovascular oscillations can become strongly coupled. The review literature describes this as a resonance effect, although the exact resonant frequency varies between individuals. Russo et al., 2017

One of the most fascinating studies for anyone interested in chanting is the famous 2001 BMJ study by Bernardi and colleagues. Twenty-three healthy adults recited either the rosary or a yoga mantra. Both rhythmic practices slowed breathing to almost exactly 6 breaths per minute and increased the synchronization of cardiovascular rhythms. Baroreflex sensitivity also increased.

Notice what this means: the study did not need to claim that a particular mantra possesses a mysterious biological frequency. The rhythm of the recitation itself organized respiration, and respiration in turn interacted with cardiovascular rhythms.

But is a longer exhale always better?

This is more nuanced.

A 2021 study compared equal inhalation/exhalation timing with a longer-exhalation condition while keeping the intrinsic breathing rate unchanged. The longer-exhalation condition increased RMSSD and high-frequency HRV, measures commonly used to assess vagally mediated cardiac modulation. Bae et al., 2021

But a 2024 study specifically tested a 1:2 inhalation-to-exhalation ratio against 1:1 breathing at 6 breaths per minute and found no significant HRV advantage for the longer exhalation. Meehan & Shaffer, 2024

So what should a chanter take away?
Slow, rhythmic breathing has substantial physiological support. A longer exhalation may be useful and may increase vagally mediated HRV in some circumstances, but science does not justify saying that “the longer the exhale, the more vagus stimulation.”

The Śloka naturally trains expiratory control

This is where Vishnu Sahasranāma becomes particularly interesting.

A long Sanskrit śloka cannot be sustained by simply pushing air out as quickly as possible. The chanter learns to meter the breath. Too much pressure at the beginning and the breath disappears early. Too little and the voice loses stability. The śloka and its chandas therefore provide a framework within which respiratory economy can be learned.

The important thing is not breath-holding. It is controlled, sustainable expiration.

03 · Rhythmic Chanting & the Vagus Nerve
Where breath becomes voice — and voice becomes neural input

Now we reach the most intriguing part.

The vagus nerve — cranial nerve X — is a major communication route between the brainstem and organs of the chest and abdomen. It carries both sensory information toward the brain and motor/autonomic signals toward visceral organs. A large proportion of vagal fibres are afferent, carrying information from organs back toward the brainstem.

The vagus is therefore not simply an “off switch.” It is a vast two-way information highway.

{needle5}

The larynx — where the vocal folds turn outgoing breath into sound — receives important innervation from branches of the vagus, including the superior laryngeal and recurrent laryngeal nerves. The neural control of vocalization is closely integrated with respiratory and autonomic brainstem circuits. González-García et al., 2024 — central autonomic mechanisms of laryngeal activity and vocalization

This is the scientifically interesting connection to chanting: when you vocalize, you are engaging a neural system that is anatomically and functionally intertwined with respiration and autonomic control.

But what exactly does the voice do to the vagus?

Here we have to be precise.

It is reasonable to say that chanting engages vagally connected laryngeal pathways and that its respiratory effects can influence autonomic regulation. It is not currently established that the vibration of the vocal folds directly “stimulates the vagus” in the same way that an implanted electrical vagus-nerve stimulator does.

In other words:

ESTABLISHED
The larynx has vagal innervation. Vocalization requires respiratory–laryngeal coordination.
SUPPORTED / PLAUSIBLE
Rhythmic vocalization can influence autonomic physiology through breathing, sensory feedback and coordinated brainstem circuits.
NOT PROVEN
That Vedic chanting directly electrically stimulates the vagus through the vocal cords or reproduces clinical VNS.

Why the OM chanting study is fascinating

Researchers at NIMHANS studied audible OM chanting using fMRI in 12 healthy volunteers. Compared with rest, OM chanting produced significant deactivation in several limbic and paralimbic regions, including the amygdala, hippocampus, anterior cingulate, insula and thalamus. A control task involving “ssss” pronunciation did not produce the same pattern. Kalyani et al., 2011

The researchers proposed a possible relationship with vagal mechanisms because of similarities with findings from vagus-nerve stimulation studies. But this was a small pilot study, and the vagal mechanism was a hypothesis, not a direct demonstration of vagal stimulation.

Why this matters for a chanter: the interesting finding is not “OM is proven to stimulate the vagus.” The interesting finding is that a structured vocal practice was associated with measurable changes in brain regions involved in emotion, attention and autonomic regulation — providing a starting point for deeper research.

The humming connection: a remarkable little experiment

Humming adds another intriguing piece.

In a 2002 experiment involving 10 healthy subjects, humming increased nasal nitric oxide approximately 15-fold compared with quiet exhalation. The proposed mechanism was oscillating airflow between the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses. Weitzberg & Lundberg, 2002

That is a genuine physiological finding. But it is important to keep the claim exactly where the evidence puts it: this was nasal NO, not proof that chanting causes a 15-fold increase in systemic nitric oxide or that it “switches off inflammation.”

Why rhythmic chanting may be powerful even without a special “vagus frequency”

  • Rhythm organizes respiration. The Bernardi study provides direct evidence for rhythmic prayer/mantra affecting breathing and cardiovascular rhythms.
  • Expiration becomes sustained. Phonation requires controlled airflow.
  • The larynx is neurally connected to the vagus. Vocalization engages vagal laryngeal pathways.
  • Auditory feedback is continuous. The chanter hears and corrects the produced sound.
  • Attention becomes rhythmically anchored. Repetition gives the mind a precise temporal object.
  • Respiration, cardiovascular activity and autonomic regulation interact. Slow breathing research provides a physiological framework for this interaction.

04 · The Enteric Nervous System
The “second brain” beneath the chant

Why should a chanting article end in the gut?

Because the nervous system does not stop at the neck.

Inside the wall of your gastrointestinal tract is an extraordinary neural network called the enteric nervous system (ENS). It contains neurons and glial cells organized into interconnected circuits that can coordinate many aspects of digestion without requiring conscious control.

This is why scientists sometimes call the ENS the “second brain.” A major review describes its complex neuronal and glial circuits and its ability to autonomously regulate bowel function. Heuckeroth et al., 2015 — Building a second brain in the bowel

The ENS is concentrated in two major plexuses: the myenteric plexus, strongly involved in gastrointestinal motility, and the submucosal plexus, involved in secretion, absorption and blood-flow regulation. Heuckeroth et al.

So how does the gut talk to the brain?

Through a remarkable gut–brain axis.

The communication is bidirectional. The brain influences the gut through autonomic, endocrine and immune pathways; the gut sends information back through vagal and spinal sensory pathways as well as hormonal, immune and metabolic signals. Carabotti et al., 2015 — gut–brain axis review

The vagus is an important route in this system. Vagal afferents carry information from the gastrointestinal tract toward the brainstem, while vagal efferent pathways influence visceral organs and interact with enteric circuits. Gut–brain axis review

Now connect this to chanting.

Suppose you sit upright and chant Vishnu Sahasranāma for 20, 30 or 60 minutes.

You repeatedly regulate your breathing.

You repeatedly extend your exhalation to complete ślokas or pādas.

You repeatedly vocalize through a laryngeal system connected to the vagus.

Your heart and breathing rhythms interact.

Your autonomic nervous system is continuously adjusting.

And the autonomic nervous system is communicating with the digestive system.

What might the gut experience?

The scientifically defensible answer is subtle.

Autonomic state influences gastrointestinal function. The ENS controls much of the local machinery. Vagal pathways connect the gut and brain. Psychological stress can alter gastrointestinal function, while gastrointestinal signals can influence brain and emotional processes.

Therefore, if a chanting practice repeatedly produces slower breathing, reduced arousal and altered autonomic dynamics, it is biologically plausible that gastrointestinal physiology could be affected indirectly.

But there is an important boundary: there is not yet sufficient direct clinical evidence to claim that Vishnu Sahasranāma chanting specifically “resets the ENS,” repairs the gut microbiome, or treats digestive disease.

The gut–brain axis is real. The ENS is real. Vagal gut–brain communication is real. The specific effects of this particular chanting practice on those systems remain an open research question.

Why the “second brain” metaphor is useful

It reminds us that the gut is not merely a tube waiting for food. It is a living sensory and computational system.

It senses. It integrates. It responds. It communicates.

And it does so continuously, whether or not we are consciously aware of it.

At every stage, information travels in both directions. The brain controls breathing and voice; breathing affects the heart; visceral organs send sensory information back toward the brain; the ENS performs local computation; and attention can modify the entire pattern.

Why chandas becomes physiologically interesting

A Vedic chandas is not merely an aesthetic arrangement of syllables. For the chanter, meter creates temporal structure. The metrical structure creates predictable śloka and pāda boundaries. Those boundaries create recurrent respiratory events. Respiratory events create rhythmic physiological oscillations.

The BMJ study on rosary prayer and yoga mantra provides a striking demonstration of this general principle: rhythmic recitation can slow respiration toward a common rate and synchronize cardiovascular rhythms. Bernardi et al., BMJ 2001

The next scientific step is therefore fascinating: rather than asking simply whether “chanting is good,” researchers can ask what respiratory signature is produced by a specific Vedic chandas?

06 · From beginner to “pro”: learn to feel the four layers

Level 1 — Just chant

Do not change anything. Simply notice: When do I inhale? How long is each śloka? Where does the breath naturally need renewal?

Level 2 — Find the diaphragm

Before chanting, spend a few minutes breathing quietly. Feel the lower ribs expand. Avoid forcing the abdomen outward. Then begin chanting and notice whether the same comfortable lower-rib movement continues.

Level 3 — Notice the exhale

Do not count yet. Simply notice how much of the śloka is carried on exhalation. You may discover that a complete śloka or sequence of pādas naturally creates a sustained expiration than the preceding inhalation.

Level 4 — Notice the heart

After a few minutes of steady chanting, become aware of the general rhythm of the body. Do not try to manufacture a “vagus sensation.” Simply observe whether the body feels more settled.

Level 5 — Notice the voice

Feel the vibration of the voice in the face, mouth, throat and chest. Listen to the sound externally as well as feeling it internally. The auditory and sensory feedback are part of the act of vocalization.

Level 6 — Notice the gut

Finally, observe the abdomen. Not as a mystical test, but as another part of the body. Does the abdomen feel tight or relaxed? Does digestion feel different before and after practice? Over weeks, patterns may become noticeable.

Where exactly is the “longer exhale”?

It is the interval during which the chanter is spending the inhaled air to realize the vocalized portion of the śloka. The lungs are not being emptied as fast as possible. Airflow is metered so that syllables remain audible and stable until the intended point of recitation. When the next breath is taken, the cycle begins again.

Where exactly is the vagus involved?

Not as a mystical line running from the sound to the gut. There are several anatomical and physiological links: the larynx is innervated by branches of the vagus; respiration interacts with cardiac autonomic control; and the vagus participates in bidirectional brain–viscera communication. These pathways form a network rather than a single switch.

And where does the enteric nervous system enter the picture?

The ENS is already operating continuously in the intestinal wall. Chanting does not need to “activate” it as though it were a dormant organ. The meaningful question is whether the respiratory and autonomic state associated with sustained rhythmic chanting changes the neural and visceral environment in which the ENS operates. That is a much more interesting — and scientifically testable — question.

09 · The deeper picture

There is a tendency to search for one magic mechanism: “The chant stimulates the vagus.”

But the physiology is much more interesting than that.

The power may be in the coordination.

A śloka gives the breath a structure.

The breath gives the voice its energy.

The voice gives the nervous system rhythmic sensory and motor input.

The respiratory rhythm interacts with cardiovascular rhythms.

The autonomic nervous system communicates continuously with visceral organs.

And deep inside the gut, the enteric nervous system continues its own extraordinary work.

So the physiological story of Vishnu Sahasranāma is not simply a story about the voice.

It is a story about rhythm becoming breath, breath becoming sound, sound becoming neural activity, and neural activity participating in the regulation of the whole organism.

For the chanter: You do not need to think about any of this while chanting. The beauty of the practice is that the physiology can unfold while attention remains with the nāma, the śloka, the chandas and the sādhana.
